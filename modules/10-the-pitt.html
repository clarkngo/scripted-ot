Section 02

Theoretical ICS Architecture

Purdue Model Impact

Level 0 — Field Devices Medical gas manifolds/cylinders, HVAC chillers and dampers, isolation-room pressure boundaries, backup generators, automatic transfer switches (ATS), patient bedside monitors.
Level 1 — Basic Control BAS controllers for HVAC/chiller sequencing and generator/ATS transfer logic; medical gas alarm panel logic per NFPA 99 — independent, hardwired circuits per gas type.
Level 2 — Supervisory / HMI Two parallel Level 2 systems whose alarms both funnel toward overlapping staff attention: the central BAS supervisory HMI (facilities control room) and the clinical central monitoring station.
Level 3 — Site Operations Hospital-wide facilities operations and emergency/incident command during a mass-casualty event.

Protocols & Control Logic

  • BACnet/MSTP or BACnet/IP: for HVAC and BAS integration.
  • NFPA 99-mandated medical gas master alarm panels: typically hardwired, independent circuits per gas type.
  • ATS transfer logic: operates on a defined power-loss-detection and transfer sequence, required to restore emergency power within 10 seconds per NFPA 110 for the applicable system type.
Section 03

The Plant — Empirical Grounding

Real-World Incident Precedent

Alarm fatigue is one of the most extensively documented patient-safety hazards in U.S. healthcare. The Joint Commission's 2013 Sentinel Event Alert #50 cited roughly 98 alarm-related patient deaths reported between 2005 and 2010, and ECRI Institute has named alarm hazards among its Top 10 Health Technology Hazards for many consecutive years. Separately, a 2022 medical gas cross-connection incident at a hospital in Sheridan, Wyoming — where a contractor cross-connected a nitrogen line into what should have been an oxygen supply during construction work — illustrates how a facility "failsafe" is only as reliable as its commissioning and source-verification procedure.

Cinematic vs. Reality Matrix

DimensionMedia Depiction (The Script)Industrial Reality (The Plant)
Failure Vector A compounding cascade — clinical surge plus simultaneous facility alarms — dramatized as a single-shift crisis. Per ECRI Institute's annual hazard reports: alarm fatigue is a chronic, everyday condition rather than an episodic crisis — clinical staff are documented to be exposed to hundreds of alarms per patient per day, the vast majority clinically insignificant, independent of any single dramatic surge event.
Time to Impact Facility-alarm consequences (chiller trip, gas pressure drop) are compressed into the same tense real-time window as the clinical crisis. Facility infrastructure failures are generally engineered with defined recovery windows (ATS within 10 seconds per NFPA 110) precisely so they don't compound acutely with a simultaneous clinical surge; per the Joint Commission, real alarm-fatigue harm is typically a single missed or desensitized alarm over a routine shift, not a dramatized multi-system cascade.
Operator Visibility Staff are shown visibly overwhelmed by competing alarm streams. Correctly reflects the documented phenomenon: the Joint Commission's alert specifically cites alarm-parameter settings not customized to individual patients, alarm signals not audible in all areas, and inconsistent staff response protocols as root visibility failures.
Failsafe Behavior Facility and clinical systems are implied to be manageable manually through the crisis by attentive staff. The Sheridan, WY cross-connection shows a failsafe gap can be catastrophic and silent — a mislabeled/miswired gas source was not caught by any automatic verification system before patient harm occurred, showing that facility "failsafes" are only as good as commissioning procedures, not real-time monitoring alone.

Root-Cause Analysis (RCA)

Classification: Human Factors / HMI Alarm Fatigue + Process Design

Primary: Human Factors / Alarm Fatigue, per the Joint Commission and ECRI Institute. Compounded, in the medical-gas grounding case specifically, by a Process Design/Commissioning failure — no independent verification of gas line identity before connection to a patient supply, a violation of NFPA 99 source-verification requirements.

Bedside monitor alarm volumeN/AECRI/Joint Commission cite ICU bedside monitors alone generating on the order of hundreds of alarms per patient per day — well beyond what's clinically actionable by any single operator

Mitigation / Recovery Protocol

  1. Alarm rationalization program (ISA-18.2 methodology): apply to both clinical monitors and BAS/facility alarm panels — eliminate default/non-customized alarm limits, classify and prioritize by clinical/operational significance.
  2. Independent annunciation paths for life-critical facility alarms: ensure medical gas and isolation-room pressure alarms cannot be masked by clinical alarm volume.
  3. Positive source verification for medical gas lines: gas-specific fittings and commissioning pressure/identity testing before any line is placed into service — directly addressing the cross-connection failure mode.
  4. Redundant, independently tested ATS/generator failover: verify the mandated sub-10-second transfer time on a routine test schedule, not only during real events.
  5. Defined escalation and manual-bypass procedures: allow facilities staff to silence/acknowledge nuisance BAS alarms without suppressing genuinely life-critical ones.

Standards Reference
